---
title: "Rebuttal: 'Bans hurt the poor'"
---

# Claim: "Banning or restricting wood burning hurts people who can't afford alternatives"

**Short answer:** In the UK this objection is largely hypothetical:
only **~4% of indoor burners rely on solid fuel for all their heating**
and only **~8% have no alternative** — most burners live in towns and
cities and are *more* affluent than average. Meanwhile the health
bill is paid by everyone else: Tasmania's half-million people carry
hundreds of millions of dollars a year in wood-smoke health costs.
The fair policy is helping the dependent minority switch — not letting
the lifestyle majority keep smoking the street.

## The evidence

**Who actually burns (UK, BEIS/Kantar 2020):**

- Only **4%** of indoor burners use solid fuel for *all* their heating;
  11% use it for most or all.
- Only **~8%** have *no* alternative heating source.
- Most burners are in **urban** areas and skew **more affluent** —
  for the large majority it's aesthetic, not essential.
→ [BEIS/Kantar, "Burning in UK Homes and Gardens" (2020)](https://naei.energysecurity.gov.uk/reports/burning-uk-homes-and-gardens-plus-number-annexes);
[Global Action Plan Domestic Burning FAQs](https://www.actionforcleanair.org.uk/evidence-resources/domestic-burning-faqs)

**The externality is a real bill:**
Researchers priced biomass-smoke health damage in Tasmania at hundreds
of millions of dollars per year — for a population of barely half a
million. Those costs fall on taxpayers, the health system, and the
neighbours' lungs.
→ Borchers-Arriagada et al., *Int. J. Environ. Res. Public Health* (2020),
[doi:10.3390/ijerph17093264](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17093264)

**Fuel poverty has a worse face:** a wood-dependent household gets the
*worst* indoor air of anyone — the stove floods the room with PM2.5 at
every refuelling. Dependence on wood is itself a health harm; switching
is the fix, not the threat.
→ Chakraborty et al., *Atmosphere* (2020),
[doi:10.3390/atmos11121326](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos11121326)

**Changeouts work — and protect the vulnerable:**
Libby, Montana's stove changeout measurably cut indoor PM2.5 in the
homes of exactly the people this argument claims to defend.
→ Noonan et al., *Indoor Air* (2012),
[doi:10.1111/j.1600-0668.2012.00789.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1600-0668.2012.00789.x)

Launceston's intervention (changeout + education, not a naked ban) cut
winter deaths ~28% (respiratory) — the mortality benefit was greatest
precisely where wood dependence was highest.
→ Johnston et al., *BMJ* (2013),
[doi:10.1136/bmj.e8446](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.e8446)

## Steelman — where the claim has a point

Genuinely wood-dependent, off-gas-grid, low-income rural households
exist — that 8% is real people, and wood can be cheap where fuel is
free or scrounged. Any serious policy needs a transition path for them:
subsidised heat pumps, fuel support, staged timelines.

## Why it still fails

- The argument defends **8%** by shielding the **92%** who have
  alternatives — most of them affluent urbanites who could switch
  tomorrow.
- The vulnerable group most harmed by wood smoke is **children and the
  already-ill** — people with no say at all. Equity cuts both ways.
- "Support the dependent minority to switch" and "let the majority
  keep burning" are different policies. The fuel-poverty objection
  argues for the first and is routinely deployed to protect the second.

## Bottom line

This is a "won't somebody think of the poor" argument deployed on
behalf of the comfortable. Defend the 8% with subsidies and
changeouts — the policy tools already proven — rather than using them
as human shields for a lifestyle choice that poisons neighbours.
