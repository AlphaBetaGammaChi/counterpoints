title: "Rebuttal: 'I burn dry wood properly'"
---

# Claim: "I burn dry, seasoned wood correctly — so my stove is fine"

**Short answer:** Burning dry wood well is the *least bad* way to do a
harmful thing — not a safe way. Even textbook operation in a modern
stove emits ~450× the PM2.5 of a gas boiler per unit of heat; moisture,
species and technique swing emissions *around that baseline*, not below
it. Good practice is harm reduction. It is not harmlessness.

## The evidence

**Best case is still orders of magnitude off.**
The emission-factor table speaks plainly: an Ecodesign stove run
*correctly* emits 335 g PM2.5/MWh vs 0.72 g/MWh for gas — and even a
pellet boiler, the best engineered biomass option, emits ~300×.
→ [UK CMO Annual Report 2022, Fig. 10](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/chief-medical-officers-annual-report-2022-air-pollution)

**Technique moves you around a bad baseline.**
Real-world emission factors vary many-fold with moisture, wood species
and operation — but the *floor* of that range is still a wood fire.
"Doing it right" chooses the better end of a terrible range.
→ McDonald et al., *Environ. Sci. Technol.* (2000),
[doi:10.1021/es9909632](https://doi.org/10.1021/es9909632)

**Smouldering is the enemy — and it happens to everyone.**
Air-starved, slumbered burns produce more and more-toxic particles.
Everyone who runs a stove overnight slumbers it; that's precisely the
mode where dry-wood virtue evaporates.
→ Kocbach Bølling et al., *Part. Fibre Toxicol.* (2009),
[doi:10.1186/1743-8977-6-29](https://doi.org/10.1186/1743-8977-6-29)

**Refuelling is inherently dirty — no technique fixes it.**
Every door opening floods the room with PM2.5 regardless of how seasoned
the wood is. It's a property of the appliance, not the fuel.
→ Chakraborty et al., *Atmosphere* (2020),
[doi:10.3390/atmos11121326](https://doi.org/10.3390/atmos11121326)

**The smoke you can't see is the worst of it.**
The most harmful fraction — ultrafine particles — is invisible. A
"clean-looking" fire tells you almost nothing about its PM2.5 output.
→ Boman et al. (2003), [doi:10.5271/sjweh.729](https://doi.org/10.5271/sjweh.729)

## Steelman — where the claim has a point

Dry wood (<20% moisture), a hot fire, good draft and a maintained stove
genuinely emit *less* than the alternative — EPA/Defra "burn right"
guidance is sound advice **for people determined to burn**. If everyone
who burns did it this way, the problem would shrink.

## Why it still fails

- "Less" is not "little". The floor under best practice is still
  hundreds of times the alternative; the difference between careless
  and careful is a multiplier on a bad number, not a fix.
- Nobody sustains perfect practice: overnight slumbering, the
  too-big load, the damp batch of logs — the measured averages in real
  homes already contain everyone's lapses.
- The defence concedes the core point: if it takes this much discipline
  to make it merely *less* harmful, the harmless version is called a
  heat pump.

## Bottom line

Seasoned wood, hot fires and a maintained stove are the difference
between very harmful and extremely harmful. The argument isn't whether
*you* burn well — it's whether anyone's burning is worth the smoke it
puts through the whole street, and on the government's own numbers the
answer doesn't change with the moisture meter.
