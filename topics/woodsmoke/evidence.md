# Wood smoke: the master evidence review

This document summarises what the peer-reviewed literature and official
statistics actually establish about residential wood smoke. Each section
ends with the key citations; the [full bibliography](references.md) links
every source.

---

## 1. What is in wood smoke

Wood smoke is not "just particles of wood". Incomplete combustion of biomass
produces a chemically complex mixture that overlaps heavily with tobacco
smoke:

- **Fine and ultrafine particles (PM2.5, PM1, UFP).** The dominant mass
  component. Small enough to reach the alveoli and pass into the
  bloodstream — the route to systemic cardiovascular harm.
- **Black carbon (BC) and brown carbon (BrC).** Light-absorbing carbon —
  a potent short-lived climate warmer on top of the health effects
  (Bond et al. 2013; Mohr et al. 2013).
- **Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)** including benzo[a]pyrene —
  mutagenic and carcinogenic.
- **Benzene, formaldehyde, acrolein** and other hazardous air pollutants —
  Schauer et al. (2001) characterised C1–C29 organic compounds from
  fireplace combustion, including dozens of species classed as hazardous.
- **Methoxyphenols and levoglucosan** — marker compounds that let
  researchers fingerprint wood smoke in ambient air (and are themselves
  respiratory irritants).
- **Dioxins and furans** — produced by incomplete combustion,
  bioaccumulative and carcinogenic.
- **Carbon monoxide, nitrogen oxides** and volatile organic compounds.

*Naeher et al. (2007)*, the standard review, concludes wood smoke "is
emitted from a source that is operationally, physically and chemically
similar to cigarette smoke" and carries many of the same toxin classes.
The International Agency for Research on Cancer classified **indoor
emissions from household combustion of biomass fuel (primarily wood) as
Group 2A — probably carcinogenic to humans** (IARC Monographs Vol. 95,
2010); emissions from household coal combustion are Group 1.

**Citations:** Naeher 2007; Schauer 2001; IARC 2010; Bond 2013; Mohr 2013.

## 2. How much is emitted — and why "renewable" ≠ "clean"

Emission factors tell the starkest story. The UK Chief Medical Officer's
2022 annual report publishes PM2.5 emission factors for domestic heating
(EMEP 2019 Guidebook basis):

| Heating method | PM2.5 emitted (g/MWh) | vs. gas boiler |
|---|---|---|
| Solid-fuel open fire | 2,950 | ~4,100× |
| Older (non-exempt) wood stove | 2,660 | ~3,700× |
| **Ecodesign / Defra-exempt stove** | **335** | **~450×** |
| Pellet-fired boiler | 216 | ~300× |
| Oil-fired boiler | 5.4 | ~7.5× |
| Gas-fired boiler | 0.72 | 1× |
| Electric heating | 0 | 0 |

Even the *best* wood appliance emits hundreds of times more PM2.5 per unit
of useful heat than gas — and infinitely more than electric heating or a
heat pump. The UK's Air Quality Expert Group estimates a single Ecodesign
stove can emit **up to six times as much PM2.5 as a Euro-6 heavy goods
vehicle**.

Laboratory ratings understate reality. McDonald et al. (2000) showed
real-world emission factors swing many-fold with wood moisture, species
and how the fire is run; Kocbach Bølling et al. (2009) showed combustion
conditions drive particle toxicity as much as fuel choice. And refuelling
is unavoidably dirty: Chakraborty et al. (2020) measured PM2.5 flooding
into UK living rooms every time the stove door opens — including in
Ecodesign/exempt appliances.

**Citations:** UK CMO 2022 / EMEP 2019; AQEG Biomass report; McDonald
2000; Kocbach Bølling 2009; Chakraborty 2020.

## 3. How much of the air pollution problem is wood smoke

The share is large, growing, and concentrated exactly where people live:

- **UK:** domestic burning is the largest single source of primary PM2.5 —
  **29% in 2022** (road transport: 18%; industrial processes: 16.5%).
  Roughly three-quarters of domestic-combustion PM2.5 is wood. It is the
  *only* major source of primary PM2.5 whose emissions have *risen* over
  the past 20 years (Defra/NAEI evidence to the Environmental Audit
  Committee; Defra 2024 statistical release: domestic combustion 20% of
  PM2.5, indoor wood burning 12%).
- **EU:** wood burning is the largest source of anthropogenic PM2.5 —
  ~51% (Glojek et al. 2022).
- **Canada:** homes burning wood emit ~21% of national PM2.5 — more than
  all on- and off-road transport combined (~12%) (Health Canada 2023).
- **London:** despite the Smoke Control Areas inherited from the Great
  Smog, wood burning is a measurable and growing share of winter particle
  pollution (Fuller et al. 2014).
- **Nordic source apportionment:** up to **81% of winter PM1** in
  Lycksele, northern Sweden (Krecl et al. 2008); ~32% of carbonaceous
  aerosol in southern Sweden (Genberg et al. 2011).
- **Switzerland:** wood burning supplies <4% of energy use yet
  contributed up to ~33% of measured black carbon (Herich et al. 2011).
- **Denmark:** in one small village, residential wood burning pushed
  particle levels to those expected beside a Copenhagen street carrying
  ~70,000 vehicles/day (Glasius et al. 2006).

It is also **concentrated in time and space**: wood smoke peaks on cold,
still winter evenings — during temperature inversions that trap it at
breathing height in residential streets, precisely when people are home.
In French Alpine valleys this has forced schools to keep children indoors
(BBC 2017). Regulatory monitors, sited away from residential chimneys,
systematically miss these hotspots: fine-scale mapping in upstate New
York found wood-smoke hotspots no regulatory monitor covers (Su et al.
2011), and neighbours of wood-burning homes can be exposed to levels
~100× what regional monitors suggest (DSAWSP).

**Citations:** Defra/NAEI 2022, 2024; Glojek 2022; Health Canada 2023;
Fuller 2014; Krecl 2008; Genberg 2011; Herich 2011; Glasius 2006;
Su 2011.

## 4. How it harms

The toxicology is consistent across study types — see
[What wood smoke does inside your body](the-body.md) for the full
mechanism walk-through.

- **Cell studies:** wood-smoke PM induces oxidative stress, DNA damage
  and inflammation at levels on a par with — or exceeding —
  traffic-derived particles (Danielsen et al. 2011).
- **Controlled human exposure:** a few hours of wood-smoke exposure
  provokes measurable airway and systemic responses — neutrophilic lung
  inflammation (Ghio et al. 2012), airway oxidative stress
  (Olin et al. 2008), coagulation and lipid-peroxidation markers
  (Barregård et al. 2006), increased arterial stiffness and reduced
  heart-rate variability within an hour (Unosson et al. 2013), and
  stronger responses in susceptible individuals (Riddervold et al. 2012).
- **Systemic translocation:** inhaled ultrafine particles reach the
  bloodstream within minutes (Nemmar et al. 2002) — the route by which
  a lung exposure becomes a cardiovascular one (Brook et al. 2010).
- **Children:** 7 of 8 studies reviewed by Larson & Koenig (1994)
  linked wood smoke to respiratory symptoms, infections and reduced
  lung function in children; the European Respiratory Society's
  position paper estimates **at least 40,000 premature deaths per year
  in Europe** from biomass smoke (Sigsgaard et al. 2015).
- **Community epidemiology:** adverse respiratory and cardiovascular
  effects in wood-burning communities occur even below legal limits
  (Boman et al. 2003).
- **Intervention evidence — the strongest kind:** when Launceston,
  Tasmania, ran a wood-heater changeout and education campaign, winter
  mortality fell ~28% (respiratory) and ~20% (cardiovascular)
  (Johnston et al. 2013). When homes in Libby, Montana, swapped old
  stoves for certified ones, indoor PM2.5 tracked burn cycles downward
  (Noonan et al. 2012).
- **Corroborating evidence from wildfire smoke** (same particle class,
  episodic rather than chronic exposure): systematic reviews link it to
  respiratory and cardiovascular harm across whole populations
  (Reid et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2015), and rising wildfire smoke is
  now erasing decades of US air-quality progress (Burke et al. 2023).
- **No safe level:** global mortality estimates show PM2.5 harm
  continuing well below current legal standards — ~8.9 million premature
  deaths in 2015 (Burnett et al. 2018). WHO estimates ~3.2 million
  deaths a year from household air pollution alone, mostly solid-fuel
  cooking and heating. The 2021 WHO Air Quality Guidelines (PM2.5:
  5 µg/m³ annual, 15 µg/m³ 24-h) explicitly state there is no evidence
  of a threshold below which no harm occurs.
- **Cost:** Tasmania's ~half-million population carries hundreds of
  millions of dollars a year in wood-smoke health costs
  (Borchers-Arriagada et al. 2020).

**Citations:** Danielsen 2011; Ghio 2012; Olin 2008; Barregård 2006;
Unosson 2013; Nemmar 2002; Brook 2010; Riddervold 2012; Boman 2003;
Larson & Koenig 1994; Zelikoff 2002; Sigsgaard 2015; Johnston 2013;
Noonan 2012; Reid 2016; Liu 2015; Burke 2023; Burnett 2018;
WHO 2021, 2024; Borchers-Arriagada 2020.

## 5. It does not stay in the burner's house

Wood smoke is literally a secondhand smoke problem at neighbourhood scale:

- Ultrafine particles infiltrate neighbouring homes through closed
  windows — typical PM2.5 infiltration efficiencies mean outdoor smoke
  largely *becomes* indoor exposure for the people next door (DSAWSP).
- The exposure gradient is hyper-local: concentrations near a
  wood-burning home can be ~100× regional background — a pollution
  footprint invisible to regulatory networks (DSAWSP; Su et al. 2011).
- Domestic solid-fuel burning also doesn't stay indoors where it starts:
  household fuel use contributes a substantial share of *ambient* PM2.5
  and the associated global disease burden (Chafe et al. 2014).

**Citations:** Chafe 2014; Su 2011; DSAWSP.

## 6. Climate — the "carbon neutral" myth is doubly wrong

- **Carbon debt:** burning wood releases *more* CO₂ per unit of energy
  up front than coal. Even under favourable forestry assumptions the
  atmospheric "carbon debt" takes decades to repay — decades in which
  the extra CO₂ warms the planet (Sterman et al. 2018).
- **Black carbon:** wood smoke is a major BC source, and BC is one of the
  most powerful short-lived climate warmers — it heats the air directly
  and darkens snow and ice, accelerating Arctic melt (Bond et al. 2013).
  California projects residential wood burning will be the state's
  largest anthropogenic BC source by 2030 (CARB 2020).
- **Brown carbon:** nitrated phenols and other wood-smoke organics absorb
  sunlight — an additional, previously overlooked warming term
  (Mohr et al. 2013).
- **Full accounting:** when short-lived climate pollutants are counted,
  Finnish wood stoves came out the *least* climate-friendly way to heat
  a house (Savolahti et al. 2019).

**Citations:** Sterman 2018; Bond 2013; Mohr 2013; Savolahti 2019;
CARB 2020.

## 7. Who burns — and who pays

The fuel-poverty objection does not match the data. The BEIS/Kantar
*Burning in UK Homes and Gardens* survey (2020) found:

- only **4%** of indoor burners rely on solid fuel for *all* their
  heating (11% for most or all);
- only **~8%** of indoor burners have *no* alternative heating source;
- most burners live in **towns and cities** and skew **more affluent** —
  for the majority it is a lifestyle choice, not a heating necessity.

Meanwhile the costs fall on people who did not choose them: neighbours,
children (whose developing lungs are most vulnerable), and the NHS.
Equitable policy means helping the small minority genuinely dependent on
solid fuel switch — subsidised heat-pump/gas conversion, as done in
successful changeout programmes (Launceston; Libby) — not granting the
majority a licence to pollute shared air.

**Citations:** BEIS/Kantar 2020; Global Action Plan 2024; Johnston 2013;
Noonan 2012; Borchers-Arriagada 2020.

---

## 8. Bottom line

1. Residential wood burning is the largest or near-largest source of
   primary PM2.5 in the UK, EU and Canada — and the only UK source still
   growing.
2. Even the cleanest wood appliance emits ~300–450× more PM2.5 than gas
   heating per unit heat; real-world operation is worse.
3. There is no known safe level of PM2.5; wood smoke carries the same
   toxin classes as tobacco smoke and is classified probably
   carcinogenic indoors (IARC 2A).
4. The exposure is communal — it infiltrates neighbouring homes and
   builds unmonitored hotspots.
5. It is bad for the climate *today* (black + brown carbon) and creates
   a decades-long carbon debt even before that.
6. When communities burn less, fewer people die. Alternatives exist for
   almost every household that burns.
